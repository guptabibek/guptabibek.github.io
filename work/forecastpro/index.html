Case study · ForecastPro

Giving pharmacy chains the analysis their ERP couldn’t.

ForecastPro sits on top of Marg ERP and turns its sales, purchase and stock data into reports, forecasts and plain-English answers. It is in production with a few large retail pharmacy chains in India. I designed and built it end to end.

Role
Product builder and sole engineer
Clients
A few large retail pharmacy chains in India
Status
In production
Built with
PostgreSQL, Redis, job queues, LLM APIs, Stripe, Docker

The problem

The chains run their business on Marg ERP. It records every sale, purchase and stock movement, but it wasn’t built for analysis: there is no way to ask questions in plain English, and little help comparing periods across the ways a pharmacy business actually slices its data.

Their teams wanted to see, every month, where sales and purchases were growing or shrinking, and why: by salesman, supplier, company, product group, salt, product, route and area.

What I built

  • ERP sync. A resumable pipeline that pulls encrypted data from Marg, stages it, and builds stock and accounting ledgers from it.
  • Growth and degrowth analysis. Month-on-month sales and purchase comparisons across nine dimensions, with drill-downs from route to city, party, product and bill.
  • Forecasting. Ten models, from moving averages and Holt-Winters to a weighted ensemble, each with a plain-language note on when to trust it.
  • Plain-English reporting. Ask a question and get a chart, table or KPI, with the same permissions as the rest of the app.
  • AI insights. Twelve checks that flag stock-out risk, customer churn, dead stock and discount anomalies, and score overall business health.
  • Operations. Expiry management, reorder suggestions, procurement, 360° reports, and PDF export and sharing.

Four decisions that shaped it

Decision 1

Make the ERP sync resumable, not just retryable.

Constraint
A first backfill for a large client runs to about a million records. Marg sends them as encrypted pages of up to about 50 MB, and each page expands several times once decoded. A single failure late in a run used to mean starting again.
Decision
Save every raw page, compressed and hash-checked, before processing it. Then move through explicit stages, each checkpointed, so a failed run resumes from the saved pages instead of fetching from Marg again. Each tenant gets one sync slot, so one client’s backfill can’t starve the others.
Trade-off
More storage and more moving parts. Worth it: a late failure now resumes from saved pages instead of forcing a full re-run.

Decision 2

Let the LLM choose what to ask, never how to query.

Constraint
SQL written by a model can be wrong, slow, or show one branch’s data to another. None of that is acceptable for financial reports.
Decision
The model only fills in a semantic query: dataset, metrics, dimensions, filters and dates, picked from a fixed catalog. My code validates it, compiles it to parameterized SQL on curated views, adds the tenant, company and branch filters, runs a safety check, executes it with a timeout and masks sensitive columns. Deterministic repairs catch the model’s common slips, such as a missing date or a “top 5 routes” question that forgot to group by route.
Trade-off
It can only answer what the catalog describes, so new kinds of questions mean extending the catalog. In return, every answer is safe, repeatable and auditable.

Decision 3

Rank by change, not by value.

Constraint
The question the chains cared about most was about change: which products, suppliers or areas fell compared with last month. Two period totals side by side don’t answer that.
Decision
A dedicated comparison path totals both periods for each dimension value and joins them so items that disappeared or newly appeared still show up. It then ranks by the signed change. A degrowth view lists only the decliners across every dimension at once, with a minimum-drop filter, and each row drills down to the parties and bills that moved it.
Trade-off
A separate query path to maintain alongside regular reports. It is also the part the clients praised most.

Decision 4

Treat AI usage like money, because it is.

Constraint
Every LLM call costs real money, and it has to be traced to the tenant that made it.
Decision
Prepaid credits on an append-only ledger that database triggers keep from being edited. Credits are reserved before each call and settled on the actual tokens used. Stripe payments credit a wallet only through a verified, idempotent webhook, and tenants can bring their own API key instead.
Trade-off
More work than a flat plan. In return, every charge traces back to a ledger row.

The result

ForecastPro is in production with a few large retail pharmacy chains in India, running on top of the ERP they already use.

They were impressed by how clearly the data is presented and visualised, and most of all by the monthly sales and purchase analysis: growth and degrowth by each dimension, and the analysis behind it.

Client feedback, paraphrased

What I’d do next

  • One bounded retry when the model returns an invalid query, feeding the validation error back to it.
  • Each tenant’s local time for every date boundary, instead of server time.
  • End-to-end tests for pinning an answer and refreshing it on a seeded database.
